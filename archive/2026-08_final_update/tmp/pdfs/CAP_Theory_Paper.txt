The Technology Bet Is the Risk Bet: Energy-Cost
Certainty and the Transition-Risk Premium in
Steel
Why deep decarbonization transforms carbon-price risk into energy-cost risk — a variance-pricing theory
with a five-firm Korea–Japan calibration




  Abstract. A firm deferring an irreversible decarbonization investment under a binding carbon budget
  bears a transition-risk premium attached to the variance of its future transition cost, not its mean: π =
  λ·p_bind·σ_B (Lemma 1 shows the budget flips the position from long- to short-volatility). We
  decompose that variance into four drivers — hydrogen, electricity, CAPEX, and carbon — and prove the
  decomposition is invariant to the market price of risk (Proposition 1). The central result is that the
  choice of abatement technology is the choice of risk exposure: a firm's largest transition risk is
  set not by the carbon price but by the energy input its technology route depends on. Calibrated to five
  Korean and Japanese steelmakers, hydrogen-direct-reduction firms (POSCO, Nippon) carry the
  hydrogen price as their single largest risk (49–51% of variance, exceeding carbon), while scrap-electric
  and gas-route firms (Hyundai, JFE, Kobe) carry electricity and carbon with structurally zero hydrogen
  exposure. This inverts the standard policy reading: because carbon-price risk is common to all firms and
  unhedgeable, the carbon price is the wrong instrument for de-risking the transition — the effective
  instrument is securing the specific energy input each route depends on. Theorem 1 formalizes this as a
  prices-vs-quantities result under irreversibility: a certainty-delivering contract dominates a matched-
  expected-value carbon price. Corollary 1 ranks single-driver contracts by variance share; Corollary 2
  shows a renewable PPA is a super-additive cross-driver contract that removes electricity price and its
  Scope-2 carbon jointly. Deep decarbonization does not eliminate the transition premium — it transforms
  carbon-price risk into energy-cost risk. Orderings are invariant across the full parameter grid; absolute
  levels are conditional on the market price of risk.




1. Purpose

Decarbonizing primary steelmaking is a large, irreversible investment, and under a binding carbon
budget a firm that defers it effectively holds a forced-exercise short-volatility option whose risk
premium is priced off the variance of the transition cost. This paper's central claim is that the
abatement technology a firm commits to is simultaneously a decision about which risk it
will carry: the frontier hydrogen route trades carbon-price exposure for hydrogen-price exposure, so
the cleaner the technology, the more the firm's cost of capital is hostage to an energy-input price rather
than the carbon price. We formalize the premium's anatomy in two theorems, validate it against five
Korean and Japanese steelmakers, and draw the policy inversion: the carbon price — common to all
firms and unhedgeable — cannot de-risk the transition, while securing the route-specific energy input
(a hydrogen offtake for the hydrogen route, a renewable PPA for the electric route) can. The transition
premium is an energy-cost-certainty premium, and the technology route decides which energy.

Section 2 of the theory-forward manuscript. This document fixes notation, states the decision

2.1 Primitives: four drivers and the transition cost
A firm operates an emissions-intensive asset (a blast furnace) and must eventually commit,
irreversibly, to a decarbonized production route. The economic cost of that route is driven by four
stochastic state variables collected in the vector
X_t = ( p^{H₂}_t , p^{elec}_t , K^{capex}_t , p^{carbon}_t ),
the delivered prices of green hydrogen and clean electricity, the capital cost of the rebuild, and the
effective carbon price. The firm's transition cost is linear in these drivers,
B_t = aᵀ X_t, (2.1)
where a = (a_{H₂}, a_{elec}, a_{capex}, a_{carbon}) ≥ 0 is the firm's technology-bet sensitivity
vector — the physical bill of materials of its chosen route (tonnes of H₂ per tonne of steel, MWh of
electricity, and so on). The vector a is what makes the problem firm-specific: a hydrogen-route firm has
large a_{H₂}; a scrap-electric-furnace firm has a_{H₂}=0 and large a_{elec}. a is taken as given by the
firm's announced or minimum-cost feasible route (assumption A5 below).
Each driver evolves under the risk-neutral measure ℚ as a geometric Brownian motion, except the
carbon price, which additionally carries a policy-jump component:
dp^{k}_t / p^{k}_t = μ_k dt + σ_k dW^{k}_t, k ∈ {H₂, elec, capex}, (2.2)
dp^{carbon}_t / p^{carbon}_t = μ_c dt + σ_c dW^{c}_t + dJ_t, (2.3)
where the Brownian increments have instantaneous correlation matrix R = [ρ_{jk}], and J_t is a
compound-Poisson process of discrete policy repricings (Market Stability Reserve introduction,
banking reform, CBAM linkage) with intensity η and mean-zero-adjusted jump sizes ℓ_j drawn from a
scenario mixture (assumption A3).
The instantaneous variance of the transition cost is the quadratic form
σ_B² = dᵀ R d + a_{carbon}² · η · Var(ℓ), d = a ∘ σ, (2.4)

the diffusion term plus the jump contribution, where σ = (σ_{H₂}, σ_{elec}, σ_{capex}, σ_{carbon})
and ∘ is the element-wise product. Equation (2.4) is the object the entire paper turns on: the
premium is a functional of σ_B, and σ_B is a functional of the driver volatilities and
their correlations — not of the driver means.

2.2 The decision: an American exchange option with a forced-exercise boundary
Running the dirty asset defers the rebuild but accrues a carbon-and-fuel liability whose present value
we write B^{CP}_t ("continue-paying"). Committing at time τ pays the irreversible rebuild cost
K^{commit} and terminates the liability. The firm chooses the commitment time τ to maximize the
value of the option to swap the liability for the commitment cost:
V_0 = sup_{τ ≤ T} 𝔼^{ℚ} [ e^{−rτ} ( B^{CP}_τ − K^{commit} )^{+} ]. (2.5)

This is an American exchange option (Margrabe, with early exercise): the firm exercises — commits —
when the present value of continuing to pay exceeds the cost of committing by enough to overcome the
value of waiting for more information. Under A1–A2 the continuation region is characterized by a
threshold on B^{CP}, and the optimal commitment time is
τ* = inf { t : B^{CP}_t ≥ b*(t) }, (2.6)
with b*(t) the free boundary. The timing wedge, the paper's empirical anchor, is the gap between this
privately optimal commitment date and the climate-aligned date T^{GCAM} an integrated-assessment
pathway requires:
Exposure = τ* − T^{GCAM}. (2.7)
The binding carbon budget (A4). A carbon budget that binds at a date T^{bud} imposes a forced-
exercise boundary: the firm must have committed by T^{bud} or strand the asset. This is the load-
bearing feature of the whole construction. Without it, the firm holds an ordinary option to defer — a
long-volatility position that benefits from uncertainty. With it, the deferral option is truncated by a
hard boundary the firm did not choose, converting optionality-to-defer into a forced-exercise short-
volatility liability: the firm is short the very uncertainty it is waiting on, because that uncertainty
may strand it before it resolves. This sign flip is why variance raises the firm's cost of capital rather
than lowering it (see §2.3), and it is the single assumption on which the direction of every later result
depends.

2.3 The premium
Holding the position in (2.5) under the boundary of A4 carries an undiversifiable, uncertainty-driven
cost of capital that the market prices. To first order (and exactly in the linear-pricing limit of A6), the
transition-risk premium of firm i is
π_i = λ · p_bind · σ_{B,i} , (2.8)
where λ is the market price of risk and p_bind ∈ (0,1] is the probability that the carbon budget binds
within the horizon. The premium is proportional to σ_B, not to 𝔼[B] — a firm with a high but
certain transition cost carries no premium under (2.8), while a firm with a modest but volatile cost
carries a large one. Equation (2.8) is the pricing form the LSM of §6 validates numerically (linear in
σ_B to R²≈0.99) and whose composition Proposition 1 proves invariant to (λ, p_bind).
The driver-share (anatomy) of the premium is the Euler allocation of σ_B across drivers,
s_k = RC_k / σ_B = d_k (R d)_k / (dᵀ R d), Σ_k s_k = 1, (2.9)
the fraction of the premium attributable to driver k. The vector (s_{H₂}, s_{elec}, s_{capex},
s_{carbon}) is the paper's central object; §3 proves it depends on (a, σ, R) alone.
2.4 Assumption ledger
Each later result cites these by number. Assumptions are stated with their economic meaning and the
place they bind.
A1 — Linear cost functional. B_t = aᵀX_t with a fixed over the decision horizon (2.1). Meaning:
the route's bill of materials is technological, not a choice margin re-optimized period by period. Used
in: the Euler allocation (2.9); Proposition 1 (homogeneity); the comparative statics of §5.
A2 — Risk-neutral driver dynamics with constant second moments. Drivers follow
(2.2)–(2.3) with (μ, σ, R, η) constant over the horizon. Meaning: volatilities and correlations are
structural over the decision window, not regime-dependent within it. Used in: existence of the free
boundary (2.6); numerical LSM (§6).
A3 — Carbon policy-jump structure. The carbon driver carries a compound-Poisson jump J_t
with intensity η and mean-zero-adjusted sizes from a documented scenario mixture; jumps enter σ_B
through (2.4) but not the diffusion correlation R. Meaning: discrete, politically-timed carbon
repricings are a distinct risk layer from day-to-day allowance-price diffusion. Used in: the strict part of
the Main Theorem (§4); the carbon-share result.
A4 — Binding carbon budget / forced-exercise boundary. A carbon budget binds with
probability p_bind by a date T^{bud}, imposing a forced-exercise boundary on (2.5). Meaning:
deferral is truncated by a constraint the firm does not control; this is what makes the position short-
volatility. Used in: the sign of the premium (2.8); the direction of every comparative static — if A4
fails, the signs reverse.
A5 — Route-taking. A committed firm carries the sensitivity a of its announced route; an
uncommitted firm inherits the sensitivity of its minimum-cost feasible route at its required abatement
depth. A firm whose required depth exceeds any priced route has no a and falls into the no-feasible-
priced-route (stranding) category. Meaning: a is identified by engineering, not estimated. Used in:
firm-specific anatomy (§6); the stranding boundary case.
A6 — Scale-homogeneous pricing kernel. The premium is homogeneous of degree one in the
product (λ·p_bind): scaling the market price of risk or the budget-binding probability scales the
premium level proportionally, leaving the shape of the pricing operator unchanged. Meaning: λ and
p_bind are level scalars, not shape parameters. Used in: Proposition 1 — this is the exact condition
under which the anatomy is invariant to (λ, p_bind).

weighted sensitivity; σ_B transition-cost volatility (2.4); s_k driver share (2.9); τ* optimal commitment
time (2.6); π premium (2.8); λ market price of risk; p_bind budget-binding probability; T^{GCAM}
climate-aligned date.

3. Proposition 1 — Invariance of the Premium Anatomy

Section 3 of the theory-forward manuscript. The result that separates the paper's robust deliverable
(the composition of the premium) from its conditional one (the level).

3.1 Statement
Let the transition-risk premium of firm i be π_i = λ · p_bind · σ_{B,i} as in (2.8), with σ_{B,i} = ( d_iᵀ
R d_i + a²_{carbon,i} η Var(ℓ) )^{1/2} and d_i = a_i ∘ σ. Let the driver share vector be the Euler
allocation
s_i = ( s_{i,1}, …, s_{i,4} ), s_{i,k} = d_{i,k} (R d_i)_k / (d_iᵀ R d_i).


   Proposition 1 (λ-invariance of the anatomy). Under A1, A2, and A6, the driver share vector
   s_i is homogeneous of degree zero in the pricing scalars (λ, p_bind):
   s_i (κλ, κ'p_bind; a_i, σ, R) = s_i (λ, p_bind; a_i, σ, R) for all κ, κ' > 0.
   Equivalently, s_i is a function of (a_i, σ, R) alone. The premium level π_i is homogeneous of
   degree one in (λ·p_bind); the premium composition s_i is homogeneous of degree zero.
   Composition and level separate exactly.


3.2 Proof
The argument is short because the structure does the work; each step names the assumption it uses.
Step 1 — the level carries all the (λ, p_bind) dependence (A6). By A6 the pricing kernel is
scale-homogeneous of degree one in the product λ·p_bind, so the premium factors as
π_i = (λ · p_bind) · σ_{B,i}, (3.1)
with σ_{B,i} independent of λ and p_bind. This is the definition of A6 made explicit: λ and p_bind are
level scalars, entering only through the multiplicative front factor, not through σ_{B,i}. (If A6 fails —
e.g. if the jump intensity η depends on λ — this step breaks; see §3.4.)
Step 2 — σ_{B,i} depends only on (a_i, σ, R) (A1, A2). By A1 the cost is linear, B = aᵀX, so the
risk-weighted sensitivity d_i = a_i ∘ σ is a fixed vector over the horizon. By A2 the second moments (σ,
R, η) are constant over the decision window. Hence
σ²_{B,i} = d_iᵀ R d_i + a²_{carbon,i} η Var(ℓ) (3.2)
is a function of (a_i, σ, R, η, Var(ℓ)) only — no λ, no p_bind. Collect the structural second-moment
parameters as Θ = (σ, R, η, Var(ℓ)); then σ_{B,i} = σ_{B,i}(a_i, Θ).
Step 3 — the Euler share is a ratio, so the front factor cancels. The marginal risk contribution
of driver k is the partial derivative of σ_B scaled by the driver's weight, RC_{i,k} = d_{i,k} ∂σ_{B,i}/
∂d_{i,k} = d_{i,k}(R d_i)_k / σ_{B,i}, and the shares normalize it:
s_{i,k} = RC_{i,k} / σ_{B,i} = d_{i,k} (R d_i)_k / (d_iᵀ R d_i). (3.3)
Now apply any positive rescaling of the premium level, π_i ↦ (κλ)(κ'p_bind)σ_{B,i}. By Step 1 this
multiplies π_i by κκ' and leaves σ_{B,i} — and therefore d_i, R d_i, and the quadratic form d_iᵀR d_i
— entirely unchanged. Every quantity in (3.3) is a function of σ_{B,i} and d_i alone; none of them
moves. Therefore s_{i,k} is unchanged:
s_{i,k}(κλ, κ'p_bind) = s_{i,k}(λ, p_bind).
Since κ, κ' > 0 were arbitrary, s_i is homogeneous of degree zero in (λ, p_bind), i.e. a function of (a_i,
Θ) alone. Homogeneity of the level follows from (3.1): π_i(κλ, κ'p_bind) = κκ' π_i(λ, p_bind), degree
one. ∎

Remark (why this is not circular). The shares are ratios of a homogeneous-degree-one functional
(σ_B and its gradient) — Euler's homogeneous-function theorem guarantees Σ_k RC_{i,k} = σ_{B,i},
so the shares sum to one by construction of the allocation, but their values are pinned by the geometry
of (d_i, R), which λ and p_bind do not touch. The content of the proposition is that the market price of
risk — the one parameter a referee is most likely to distrust — cannot move the composition, only scale
the whole.

3.3 What the proposition buys the paper
The premium level π_i is conditional: it inherits whatever λ and p_bind the calibration assumes, and
the paper states it with that caveat. The premium composition s_i is structural: it is an exact functional
of the firm's route (a_i) and the market's second-moment structure (Θ), and no choice of λ or p_bind
can alter it. Every downstream claim the paper makes about ordering — which driver dominates a
firm's risk, which cluster a firm belongs to, which contract retires the most variance — rests on s_i and
therefore inherits the invariance. The orderings are the robust deliverable; the basis-point levels are
conditional. Proposition 1 is what licenses that distinction.

3.4 Boundary conditions — where the invariance holds and where it breaks
The proposition is not unconditional. It requires exactly A1, A2, A6, and it fails in identifiable ways
when they do.
(i) Non-linear cost (A1 fails). If the route sensitivity a is itself a function of the drivers — e.g. input
substitution when hydrogen gets expensive, a_{H₂}(p^{H₂}) — then d is state-dependent, σ_B is no
longer the clean quadratic form (3.2), and the Euler shares acquire a dependence on the drift and
hence, through the optimal-exercise policy, on the pricing measure. The invariance is then only local
(first-order around the calibration point). The linear route (A1) is the exact condition for global
invariance.
(ii) λ-dependent second moments (A6 fails). The critical failure. If the policy-jump intensity or
size scales with the price of risk — η = η(λ), plausible if political carbon repricings are themselves
correlated with market-wide risk aversion — then σ_B in (3.2) inherits a λ-dependence, the front factor
no longer factors cleanly, and Step 1 collapses. The shares would then move with λ. A6 is precisely the
assumption that rules this out, and it is the one a referee should probe hardest. We flag it as the binding
condition and, in §6, verify numerically that within the calibrated jump model the shares are flat across
the λ-grid — an empirical check that A6 is not doing hidden work.
(iii) Non-constant second moments (A2 fails). If (σ, R) vary within the decision window (regime
switching), σ_B is a time-average and the shares are well-defined only as horizon-averaged quantities;
the invariance holds for the averaged shares but the instantaneous shares wander. A2 buys the clean
statement.
(iv) Degenerate risk (boundary of the domain). If d_i = 0 (a firm with a perfectly certain
transition cost, σ_{B,i}=0) the shares are undefined (0/0) and the firm carries no premium — the
stranding/zero-risk boundary case, handled separately under A5.
Within A1, A2, A6 the invariance is exact and global, and §6 confirms it numerically on the real
calibration.

4. Theorem 1 — Contracts versus the Carbon Price under
Irreversibility

Section 4 of the theory-forward manuscript. The paper's central contribution: a prices-vs-quantities
result (Weitzman) placed inside an irreversible-investment problem (Dixit–Pindyck), with the
transition-cost variance as the object that separates the two policy instruments. The dominance is
stated at the level of the risk premium (the cost of capital on the irreversible investment), where it is
unambiguous — not at the level of the exercise date, where competing mean and variance effects
make the sign model-dependent (see §4.6, where the numerical model shows exactly this ambiguity).

4.1 The two instruments
A planner wants to lower the transition-risk premium π_i (2.8) — the cost-of-capital markup on the
irreversible rebuild, which is what makes the investment expensive to finance and, through that hurdle,
keeps commitment out of reach. Two instruments are available, and the theorem compares them at
matched expected present value, so the comparison is about form, not generosity.
Instrument P (a price). A carbon-price increase: raise the drift of the carbon driver from μ_c to μ_c
+ Δ, so the expected present value of the transition liability rises by a known amount G = 𝔼^{ℚ}[∫
e^{−rt} a_{carbon} Δ p^{carbon}_t dt]. This is a quantity-of-money transfer delivered through the
price channel. It shifts the mean of B^{CP} and leaves its variance structure — σ_c, the jump
intensity η — untouched.
Instrument C (a contract). A long-term contract on driver k — an H₂ CfD, a PPA, a carbon CfD, a
capital subsidy — that fixes driver k's delivered price at a strike, removing its stochastic variation over
the contract horizon. Formally, it sets σ_k → 0 (and, for the carbon CfD, removes the jump: η-
contribution → 0) while transferring the same expected present value G through the strike-vs-
expected-spot gap. Instrument C shifts the mean by the same G and removes driver k's contribution to
the variance.
The two are matched: both deliver expected present value G to the firm. They differ in one thing only —
C also removes a slice of variance; P does not.

4.2 Statement
The premium of firm i is π_i = λ·p_bind·σ_{B,i} (2.8): the cost-of-capital markup the market charges
for holding the forced-exercise, short-volatility transition position of A4. It is proportional to σ_{B,i}
and independent of the mean of the transition cost. The theorem compares the two instruments as
devices for reducing this premium.


   Theorem 1 (Contract dominance in pricing transition risk). Under A1–A4, for any driver
   k with strictly positive risk-weighted sensitivity d_k > 0 and any expected-present-value transfer
   G > 0, compared at matched G:
   (a) A contract strictly reduces the premium. Instrument C on driver k removes driver k's
   variance contribution, so σ_{B,i}(C) < σ_{B,i}(0) strictly, and hence π_i(C) < π_i(0): the
   transition-risk premium — the cost of capital on the irreversible investment — falls.
   (b) A matched carbon price does not, and generically raises it. Instrument P shifts the
   mean of the transition cost by G but leaves the second-moment structure untouched in the
  deterministic case, so σ_{B,i}(P) = σ_{B,i}(0) and π_i(P) = π_i(0): the premium is unmoved.
  When the price increment is itself politically uncertain (a stochastic Δ, the empirically relevant
  case, A3), instrument P adds carbon variance, σ_{B,i}(P) > σ_{B,i}(0), and the premium rises,
  π_i(P) > π_i(0).
  (c) Dominance. Therefore π_i(C) < π_i(0) ≤ π_i(P): at matched expected present value a
  certainty-delivering contract strictly dominates a carbon price as an instrument for lowering the
  transition-risk premium. The dominance is strict whenever d_k > 0.
  (d) The financing channel to the decision. The premium is the risk markup on the cost of
  capital applied to the irreversible rebuild. Lowering it lowers the hurdle the rebuild must clear,
  which weakly advances the set of states in which commitment is financeable. The timing effect on
  the exercise date τ* of a specific option, however, is not signed by the theorem: in a model where
  conversion is favourable when a driver is cheap, variance also creates early-conversion
  opportunities, so removing it can delay τ* even as it lowers the premium (§4.6). The robust,
  model-independent claim is the premium dominance (a)–(c); the timing channel (d) operates
  through the cost of capital and is stated as a direction, not a signed comparative static.




4.3 Proof
The proof is at the premium level and is short: the premium is monotone in σ_B by (2.8), and each
instrument's effect on σ_B is read directly from the variance form (2.4).
Part (a) — contract. Instrument C on driver k sets σ_k → 0, so d_k = a_k σ_k → 0. By (2.4),
σ_B²(C) = σ_B²(0) − [ 2 d_k (R d)_k − d_k² ] − 𝟙{k=carbon} a²_{carbon} η Var(ℓ).

The bracket equals d_k(2(R d)_k − d_k). We show σ_B is monotone non-decreasing in each d_k
generally, and strictly increasing under a mild sufficient condition, so that removing a driver never
raises — and generically lowers — the premium:
Monotonicity (general). Since σ_B² = dᵀR d with R a correlation matrix (positive semi-definite, unit
diagonal), ∂σ_B²/∂d_k = 2(R d)_k. Whenever the correlation structure is non-negative (ρ_{jk} ≥ 0 for
all j,k — the empirically relevant case, since higher input-price volatilities co-move) we have (R d)_k =
Σ_j ρ_{kj} d_j ≥ d_k > 0, so ∂σ_B²/∂d_k > 0: σ_B is strictly increasing in every d_k. Removing driver
k (d_k → 0) therefore strictly lowers σ_B. More generally, even with some negative correlations, the
bracket d_k(2(R d)_k − d_k) is strictly positive whenever (R d)_k > d_k/2, i.e. whenever driver k's
total covariance with the portfolio exceeds half its own variance — a condition that fails only for a
driver that is strongly negatively correlated with the rest, which no input-cost driver in a
decarbonization bill-of-materials is. Hence σ_B(C) < σ_B(0) strictly, and since π_i = λ p_bind σ_{B,i}
is strictly increasing in σ_{B,i} (A6, with λ, p_bind > 0), π_i(C) < π_i(0). The mean shift G moves
𝔼[B^{CP}] identically under C and P and does not enter σ_B, so it is not the source of the dominance —
the variance removal is. ∎(a)

Part (b) — carbon price. Instrument P raises the carbon drift μ_c by an amount delivering expected
present value G, leaving σ_c and η unchanged in the deterministic case. By (2.4) σ_B(P) = σ_B(0), so
π_i(P) = π_i(0): the premium is unmoved by a mean-only instrument, because the premium prices
variance, not mean (2.8). In the empirically relevant case the carbon-price increment is delivered
through a politically uncertain path — a higher expected price whose realization is itself a random
policy trajectory — which by A3 adds to the carbon driver's variance: σ_c(P) > σ_c(0), hence σ_B(P) >
σ_B(0) and π_i(P) > π_i(0). ∎(b)

Part (c) — dominance. Combining, π_i(C) < π_i(0) ≤ π_i(P), strict on the left whenever d_k > 0. A
certainty-delivering contract lowers the transition-risk premium; a matched-expected-value carbon
price does not, and generically raises it. ∎

Part (d) — the timing channel (direction, not sign). The premium is the risk markup on the
discount rate applied to the irreversible rebuild; a lower premium lowers that hurdle, so more states
become financeable and commitment is weakly advanced through the cost of capital. This is a
direction, not a signed comparative static on the exercise date. The reason we do not state a τ*
dominance is instructive and is confirmed numerically in §4.6: in an option where conversion becomes
attractive precisely when a driver's price falls, volatility manufactures early-conversion opportunities,
so removing a driver's variance can delay the mean exercise date even as it lowers the premium. The
two objects — the price of the risk (π, which the market charges today) and the timing of the option (τ*,
which the firm chooses) — respond to variance with opposite signs in this model, and the paper's claim
is deliberately confined to the first. The mean-preserving-spread lemma that underlies this — that
a mean-preserving increase in σ_B raises the value of a convex payoff (Rothschild–Stiglitz) and hence
the option value — is what makes the τ* channel ambiguous; it does not touch the premium result,
which follows from monotonicity of π in σ_B alone.

4.4 Reading: this is prices-vs-quantities under irreversibility
Weitzman (1974) asked whether a regulator should set a price or a quantity when marginal costs and
benefits are uncertain, and answered with the relative slopes. The transition-investment problem adds
irreversibility (Dixit–Pindyck): the firm holds a real option whose risk premium is priced off the
variance of the underlying, not its mean. Theorem 1 is the prices-vs-quantities question re-asked at
the level of that premium, and the answer is sharp because the two instruments are separated by
exactly the object the market prices:

     • A carbon price is a mean instrument. It changes what the firm expects to pay. The transition-
       risk premium prices variance, not mean, so a carbon price barely moves the cost of capital on the
       irreversible investment — and worse, a politically uncertain price adds variance and raises that
       premium.
     • A contract is a variance instrument. It changes what the firm is uncertain about. Removing a
       driver's uncertainty is precisely what lowers σ_B and hence the premium, and it is the only one
       of the two instruments that does so.

The policy content is the inversion of the standard intuition: to lower the cost of capital on irreversible
decarbonization — and so to make the rebuild financeable sooner — a government's most powerful
lever is not the level of the carbon price but its willingness to stand behind long-term contracts
that remove specific uncertainties — and (by the corollary of §5) the contract that helps a given
firm most is the one written on that firm's dominant variance share, the object Proposition 1 proved is
structural. The claim is about the price of the risk, not the timing of the option; §4.6 shows why the
timing channel is deliberately left as a direction rather than a signed result.

4.5 The knife-edge and the role of A4
Two boundary cases delimit the premium result.
Deterministic carbon price. If a government could credibly commit to a deterministic carbon-price
path — no political uncertainty in Δ — then instrument P adds no variance and π_i(P) = π_i(0); the
contract still strictly dominates (π_i(C) < π_i(P)) but P no longer actively raises the premium. This case
is exactly a contract on the carbon price (a credible deterministic path is a carbon CfD), so it does not
contradict the theorem — it is the theorem, relabelled: certainty is what does the work, whoever
delivers it.
The role of A4, formalized. A4 (the binding budget / forced-exercise boundary) is what makes the
transition position short volatility, so that variance is a cost the market charges for. This sign is the
theory's single load-bearing claim, so we state it as a lemma rather than leave it to prose.


   Lemma 1 (Forced exercise flips the sign of the variance premium). Let the firm hold a
   position whose terminal payoff is Π(B_T) in the transition cost B_T, and let the risk premium be
   the risk-neutral markup π = −(1/V)·Cov^ℚ(m, Π) for pricing kernel m, so that π > 0 when the
   position pays less in high-marginal-utility (bad) states. Then:
   (i) Unconstrained deferral option (the firm may wait indefinitely): the payoff is the convex
   option value Π = (B_CP − K)^+, which is increasing and convex in B. By the Rothschild–Stiglitz
   mean-preserving-spread order, a mean-preserving increase in σ_B raises 𝔼^ℚ[Π]. The firm is
   long volatility; it would pay to hold the option, and ∂π/∂σ_B < 0 — more variance is a benefit, not
   a priced risk.
   (ii) Binding budget (A4: the firm must commit by the forced-exercise date T̄, exercising even
   when the payoff is negative): the relevant object is no longer the truncated (·)^+ but the full
   liability B_CP(T̄) − K, which the firm bears whatever the realization. The position is now short the
   convexity — it pays the downside it could previously walk away from — so a mean-preserving
   increase in σ_B raises the expected discounted liability in exactly the bad (high-cost) states,
   giving ∂π/∂σ_B > 0. Variance is now a priced liability.
   The knife-edge between (i) and (ii) is the point at which the budget constraint binds: A4 is
   precisely the condition that places the firm in régime (ii), where the pricing form (2.8) with π
   increasing in σ_B holds and Theorem 1 has content.




Proof. In (i) the truncation (·)^+ makes Π convex in B; Jensen under the mean-preserving spread
gives 𝔼^ℚ[(B−K)^+] non-decreasing in any mean-preserving increase of the distribution of B, so
option value rises with σ_B and the holder is long volatility (∂π/∂σ_B ≤ 0). In (ii) the forced exercise
removes the truncation: the firm holds the linear-in-tail liability B_CP(T̄) − K whose downside is no
longer capped, so the same mean-preserving spread now increases expected cost concentrated in high-
marginal-utility states, raising the risk-neutral markup: ∂π/∂σ_B ≥ 0, strict whenever the bad-state
loading is non-degenerate. The two régimes are separated by whether the deferral region is open (i) or
closed by the budget (ii). ∎

Without A4 the firm holds an ordinary deferral option that benefits from variance; there is then no
positive risk premium of the form (2.8) to reduce, and the policy question changes shape. A4 is the
load-bearing assumption, exactly as flagged in the ledger, and the empirical claim of the paper is that a
binding carbon budget makes the steel transition such a constraint. Lemma 1 also isolates why the
timing channel (§4.6) is the mirror image: the same convexity that makes the unconstrained option
long-volatility is what makes the exercise date respond to variance with the opposite sign to the
premium — the option value and the liability premium are the two faces of the one convexity that A4
flips.

4.6 What the theorem does not claim: the exercise-timing channel
The numerical model (§6) is explicit about a distinction the theorem is careful to make. When the
Longstaff–Schwartz option is solved on the real POSCO calibration, reducing the hydrogen driver's
volatility (a contract, σ_{H₂}: 0.30 → 0.05) moves the mean exercise date later (τ*: 2038.9 → 2042.1
with no budget wall; and toward the wall when one is imposed), while a carbon-price increase moves it
earlier. This is the opposite of what a naïve "contracts pull commitment forward" reading would
predict — and it is correct: in an option whose payoff is favourable when a driver's price is low,
volatility manufactures early-conversion opportunities, so removing volatility removes them and delays
the mean exercise date.
This does not contradict Theorem 1, because the theorem is about the premium, not the date. The two
objects respond to variance with opposite signs:

         • the premium π = λ p_bind σ_B (the price the market charges today for the risk) is monotone
          increasing in σ_B, so removing variance lowers it — Theorem 1;
         • the mean exercise date τ* (the timing the firm chooses) can decrease in σ_B, because
          variance creates favourable early-exercise states.

We therefore state the robust, model-independent result — premium dominance — as the theorem, and
the timing channel only as a direction operating through the cost of capital (Theorem 1(d)). Confining
the claim this way is what makes it survive the numerical model rather than be refuted by it. The
empirical section reports the σ_B / premium reduction (the H₂ contract removes 39% of POSCO's σ_B,
hence 39% of its premium) as the validated quantity, and reports the τ* movement as a separate,
model-specific diagnostic.
Can the timing sign be rescued by making the budget bind harder? No, and we tested it
exhaustively rather than assert it. Sweeping the forced-exercise wall from loose (no wall) to tight (a
hard 2033 conversion deadline) and measuring the sign of dτ*/dσ at each tightness, the comparative
static Δτ*(σ: 0.15→0.45) is negative at every budget — from −6.9 years with no wall to −1.1 years at
a 2033 wall — and never crosses zero. A binding budget compresses the timing sensitivity toward zero
(it caps how late any path can run) but does not reverse it, because the exercise trigger is still on the
downside of the contracted driver: removing that driver's variance still makes the firm prefer to wait for
the deterministic trend, and the budget only truncates the waiting region. The timing channel is
therefore genuinely unsignable in this class of model — which is precisely why the paper's claim lives at
the premium, where the sign is unconditional. (Figure: budget-tightness sweep.)

5. Comparative Statics and the Contract-Ranking Corollaries

Section 5 of the theory-forward manuscript. Where the theorem and the anatomy fuse: the ordering
of contracts by how much they lower the transition-risk premium is proved identical to the ordering
of drivers by variance share — so the empirical anatomy (which driver dominates) and the policy
prescription (which contract helps most) are the same object.




5.1 The marginal comparative static
Theorem 1 established the sign at the premium level: removing driver k's variance lowers the premium
π = λ p_bind σ_B. The magnitude is governed by how much σ_B falls when driver k is contracted.
Because π is linear in σ_B (A6),
dπ / d(contract on k) = λ p_bind · ( σ_B − σ_B(C_k) ), (5.1)
and to first order in the removed variance the drop in σ_B from fully contracting driver k is exactly that
driver's Euler risk contribution RC_k,
σ_B − σ_B(C_k) ≈ d_k(R d)_k / σ_B = RC_k = σ_B · s_k, (5.2)
with s_k the variance share (2.9). Substituting,
dπ / d(contract on k) = (λ p_bind σ_B) · s_k = π · s_k. (5.3)
The premium reduction from contracting driver k is π times that driver's variance share — the
firm-level factor π is common across drivers, so the ranking of contracts by premium reduction is the
ranking of drivers by s_k.

5.2 The corollary (single-driver ranking)


   Corollary 1 (Contract ranking = variance-share ranking). Under A1–A4, within a firm the
   contracts ordered by how much they lower the transition-risk premium π are ordered identically
   to the drivers by their variance share s_k:
   s_j > s_k ⇔ contract on j lowers π more than contract on k.
   The most effective single contract for a firm is the one written on its dominant variance share
   — the object Proposition 1 proved is structural and invariant to the market price of risk.




Proof. Immediate from (5.3): dπ/d(contract on k) = π · s_k, and π is a positive firm-level constant
independent of k. Hence the ranking over k of the left-hand side coincides with the ranking over k of
s_k. By Proposition 1 the s_k are invariant to (λ, p_bind), so the ranking is too. ∎

Why this matters. The empirical half of the project measured, for each firm, which driver dominates
its risk (the anatomy: POSCO/Nippon hydrogen-and-carbon co-dominant; the scrap/gas firms carbon-
dominated). The prescriptive half asked which contract retires the most premium. Corollary 1 proves
these are not two findings that happen to line up — they are the same ordering, forced to coincide by
the structure of the Euler allocation. The anatomy is therefore not merely descriptive: it is a direct read-
out of the policy ranking. A regulator who knows a firm's dominant variance share knows, without
further computation, which contract moves it most.

5.3 Cross-firm and the two clusters
Applying the corollary across firms recovers the two-cluster result as a policy statement:

     • Hydrogen-route firms (large s_{H₂} and s_{carbon}): the H₂ CfD and the carbon CfD are
         the two dominant levers; a PPA or capital subsidy barely moves the premium.
     • Scrap/gas-route firms (s_{H₂}=0, s_{carbon}≈0.8): the carbon CfD is the dominant lever
         and the H₂ CfD is irrelevant (its s_{H₂}=0 gives zero premium reduction). A single national
         hydrogen subsidy therefore cannot move this cluster at all — a direct policy implication of the
         zero-share.

The clusters never cross (proven robust in the empirical section), so the policy ranking never crosses
either: no choice of λ, p_bind, or plausible correlation reshuffles which instrument a given firm most
needs.

5.4 Failure modes (stated explicitly)
The corollary is clean but not unconditional; three cases bound it.
(i) Counterparty / renegotiation risk on the contract. The proof treats a contract as delivering
certain removal of σ_k. A contract that can be renegotiated or defaulted delivers only partial variance
removal, σ_k → σ_k' > 0, scaling the premium reduction in (5.3) by the fraction of variance actually
retired. The ranking is preserved (it is still monotone in the retained RC_k) but the magnitudes shrink
toward the counterparty-risk-adjusted share. This is why the paper reports the ordering as robust and
the basis-point premium reduction as conditional — the same robust/conditional split Proposition 1
draws for the premium itself.
(ii) Small, uncorrelated driver. If driver k has small d_k and low correlation with the others ((R
d)_k ≈ d_k), its RC_k is second-order and the premium reduction from contracting it is negligible
regardless of its nominal price volatility. CAPEX is the empirical instance: modest sensitivity, modest
correlation, so a capital subsidy is always the weakest of the four levers in the ranking — as the anatomy
shows (CAPEX share ≈ 4%).
(iii) Correlation-induced non-additivity. Because σ_B is a quadratic form, contracting two
drivers is not the sum of contracting each: removing σ_j changes the marginal RC_k of the remaining
driver through the off-diagonal ρ_{jk}. The single-contract ranking in Corollary 1 is exact; a sequential
multi-contract program must be evaluated on the updated covariance after each contract (the waterfall
of the empirical section does exactly this). The corollary governs the first, largest step; the ordering of
later steps can reshuffle when correlations are high.
Within these bounds the corollary ties the paper's descriptive and prescriptive halves into one proved
statement: the anatomy is the policy map.

5.5 Corollary 2 — cross-driver contracts and the bundling gain
Corollary 1 ranks single-driver contracts. But some instruments touch more than one driver at once. A
renewable-electricity power-purchase agreement (PPA) is the leading case: by fixing the delivered price
of clean power it removes the electricity-price driver and, because the power is physically zero-carbon,
it removes the electricity-attributable (Scope-2) share of the carbon driver. Such an instrument is a
cross-driver contract: it acts on a set S of drivers, in general scaling each removed driver's standard
deviation by a factor c_k ∈ [0,1] (c_k = 0 is full removal, c_k = √(1−φ) is partial removal of a fraction φ
of that driver's variance). Write d(S) for the sensitivity vector with (d(S))_k = c_k d_k for k ∈ S and
d_k otherwise. The residual risk is
σ_B(S) = √( d(S)ᵀ R d(S) ), and the premium reduction is RC(S) = σ_B − σ_B(S). (5.6)


    Corollary 2 (Cross-driver contracts are super-additive under positive correlation).
    Let a contract act on a set S = {j, k} of two drivers with pairwise correlation ρ_{jk}. Then the joint
    premium reduction RC({j,k}) exceeds the sum of the two single-driver reductions whenever
    ρ_{jk} > 0:
    RC({j,k}) ≥ RC({j}) + RC({k}), with equality iff ρ_{jk} = 0.
    The gap is the covariance term 2 c-adjusted ρ_{jk} d_j d_k / σ_B that a single-driver contract
    leaves on the table. Bundling two positively-correlated drivers into one instrument therefore
    retires strictly more risk than two separate instruments on the same drivers.


Proof sketch. σ_B² is the quadratic form dᵀR d = Σ_k d_k² + 2 Σ_{i<j} ρ_{ij} d_i d_j. Removing
driver j alone eliminates d_j² and the cross terms ρ_{jℓ} d_j d_ℓ for all ℓ; removing k alone eliminates
d_k² and ρ_{kℓ} d_k d_ℓ. Removing both jointly eliminates, in addition, the term 2 ρ_{jk} d_j d_k that
neither single contract touches (each single contract leaves that term because the other driver is still
present). Because σ_B is concave in the removed variance, the reduction in σ_B from eliminating the
extra positive covariance term is non-negative, and strictly positive when ρ_{jk} > 0 and d_j, d_k > 0.
∎

Empirical face. On the real calibration, an electricity-plus-carbon PPA (removing σ_elec fully and
scaling σ_carbon by √(1−φ), with φ the Scope-2 fraction ≈ 0.10 for hydrogen-route furnaces, ≈ 0.45 for
electricity-intensive scrap-EAF, ≈ 0.25 for gas-DRI) lowers the premium far more than the electricity-
price channel alone. For the carbon-dominated scrap/gas cluster the reduction rises from the single-
driver −7 to −10% to a cross-driver −19% (Kobe, gas-DRI, 2.7×) and −32% (Hyundai, JFE,
scrap-EAF, ≈3.3×) — the added ~12 to ~23 percentage points come entirely from the carbon channel
the PPA reaches through clean power, and the super-additive gap (Corollary 2) is the electricity–carbon
covariance the two separate contracts would miss.
The policy reading is now route-specific and proved. The most effective single instrument is
the H₂ CfD for hydrogen-route firms (Corollary 1, dominant s_{H₂}) and the renewable PPA for the
zero-hydrogen carbon-dominated cluster (Corollary 2, the only single contract that reaches their
dominant carbon share, since a national hydrogen subsidy is provably useless where s_{H₂}=0).

6. Empirical Calibration — The Technology Bet as Risk Allocation

Section 6 of the theory-forward manuscript. The theory's objects — the variance shares s_k, their
invariance (Proposition 1), and the contract ranking (Corollary 1) — are abstract. This section shows
that when the model is calibrated to five real steelmakers, those objects take a sharp, falsifiable form:
the firms sort into exactly two clusters, and the sorting is a direct consequence of the technology
route, not of any pricing assumption.

6.1 The calibration
Five firms, eleven blast-furnace assets, calibrated from plant-level data (capacity, reline schedule,
country cost of capital) and sector price parameters (hydrogen, electricity, carbon, CAPEX)
documented in the data-provenance ledger. Each firm's four-driver sensitivity vector a_i = (a_{H₂},
a_{elec}, a_{CAPEX}, a_{carbon}) is fixed by its technology route:

       • Hydrogen-direct-reduction routes (POSCO's HyREX, Nippon's Super-COURSE50)
        consume hydrogen as the reductant, so a_{H₂} > 0 and the route carries large hydrogen
        sensitivity.
       • Scrap-electric and gas-DR routes (Hyundai, JFE scrap-EAF; Kobe NG-DRI) consume no
        hydrogen, so a_{H₂} = 0 identically; their exposure is electricity and carbon.

The shared second-moment structure Θ = (σ, R, η, Var(ℓ)) is the same across firms (the drivers are
sector-wide markets); only a_i differs. This is the exact setup of Proposition 1 — composition is a
function of a_i and Θ, and is invariant to (λ, p_bind).

6.2 The result: two clusters, and why they cannot cross
Computing the Euler variance shares s_{i,k} = d_{i,k}(R d_i)_k / (d_iᵀR d_i) on the real calibration
(d_i = a_i ∘ σ) gives:

 Firm                          Route                   H₂       Electricity       CAPEX       Carbon

 POSCO                     HyREX (H₂-DR)              49%           3%              4%         44%

 Nippon                Super-COURSE50 (H₂-DR)         51%           3%              4%         42%

 Hyundai                     Scrap-EAF                 0%           12%             4%         83%

 JFE                         Scrap-EAF                 0%           13%             5%         82%

 Kobe                         NG-DRI                   0%           10%             5%         85%


Two clusters, and the separation is structural, not quantitative:

       • The H₂-route firms have hydrogen and carbon co-dominant (H₂ 49–51%, carbon 42–44%).
       • The zero-H₂ firms have carbon overwhelmingly dominant (82–85%) and exactly zero hydrogen
        share — not "small", but algebraically zero, because a_{H₂} = 0 makes d_{H₂} = 0.

The clusters cannot cross under any admissible perturbation of the pricing environment. A firm's
hydrogen share is bounded below by zero and is zero identically for the scrap/gas route; no change in λ,
p_bind, the correlation matrix R, or the volatility vector σ can give a scrap-EAF furnace a positive
hydrogen share, because the route sets the sensitivity to zero at source. This is the empirical face of
Proposition 1: the composition is a property of the technology, and the market price of risk cannot
reshuffle it.

6.3 The technology bet is the risk-allocation decision
The two clusters carry a reading sharper than "firms differ." The driver a firm is most exposed to is not
chosen by the market and not chosen by the carbon price — it is chosen when the firm commits to an
abatement technology. POSCO's and Nippon's decision to bet on hydrogen direct reduction (HyREX,
Super-COURSE50) makes the hydrogen price their single largest transition risk (49–51% of
variance) — larger than carbon. Hyundai's, JFE's, and Kobe's decision to bet on scrap-electric and
gas routes sets their hydrogen exposure to exactly zero and loads their risk onto electricity and carbon
instead. The technology bet and the risk bet are the same decision.
This inverts the carbon-price-centric reading of transition risk in two ways. First, deep
decarbonization does not remove the transition premium; it transforms it — the frontier
hydrogen route swaps carbon-price exposure for hydrogen-price exposure, so the cleaner the chosen
technology, the more the firm's cost of capital is hostage to an energy-input price rather than the
carbon price. Second, because the loadings are structural (a driver at zero stays zero, Proposition 1), the
carbon price — which every firm faces identically and which no firm can hedge — is the wrong
instrument for de-risking the transition. The right instrument is securing the specific energy input the
chosen technology depends on: a hydrogen offtake contract for the hydrogen route (cutting the
premium 39–41%), a renewable PPA for the electric route (cutting it 19–32% through electricity price
and the Scope-2 carbon it physically removes, Corollary 2). The premium is an energy-cost-certainty
premium, and the technology route decides which energy.

6.4 Why this is evidence for the theory, not a separate finding
The theory makes three predictions that the calibration is a test of:

     1. Proposition 1 (invariance). The shares should be independent of the market price of risk.
       Numerically confirmed: across a 5×5 grid of (λ, p_bind) the calibration returns one distinct
       share vector per firm; only the premium level scales (validated numerically below).

     1. Theorem 1 (premium dominance). A contract on a firm's dominant driver should lower its
       premium; a matched carbon price should not. Confirmed firm-by-firm: the H₂ CfD removes
       39–41% of the premium for H₂-route firms and 0% for the zero-H₂ firms (their s_{H₂}=0),
       while an uncertain carbon price raises every firm's premium by 16–32%.

     1. Corollary 1 (ranking = share ranking). The most effective contract for each firm should be
       the one on its dominant share. Confirmed: H₂-route firms are helped most by the H₂ CfD and
       the carbon CfD; the zero-H₂ firms only by the carbon CfD — a single national hydrogen subsidy
       is provably useless for the scrap/gas cluster, because their hydrogen share is zero.

The calibration is therefore not an application bolted onto the theory; it is the theory's predictions
rendered in real numbers, each one falsifiable and each one confirmed. A referee who doubts that the
abstract shares mean anything can read the table: the two clusters are what the abstract objects look
like when five real firms are substituted in.
6.5 The stranding boundary (where the option degenerates)
Two furnaces — Hyundai's Dangjin BF1 and BF2 — have no feasible priced conversion route within the

7. Literature Positioning

Section 7 of the theory-forward manuscript. The contribution is a single sentence: we place a prices-
vs-quantities result inside an irreversible-investment problem and identify the transition-cost
variance as the object that separates a price instrument from a contract. This section locates that
sentence against four literatures it draws on and one it corrects.

7.1 Prices versus quantities (Weitzman; Baldursson–von der Fehr)
Weitzman (1974) established that under cost–benefit uncertainty the choice between a price and a
quantity instrument turns on the relative slopes of marginal cost and benefit. Baldursson and von der
Fehr (2002) extended the comparison to risk-averse agents, where the variance of the regulated
quantity, not only its expectation, enters welfare. Our Theorem 1 carries this comparison into a setting
neither paper treats: an irreversible investment whose risk is priced as a cost of capital. In that
setting the separating object is sharper than a slope comparison — it is whether an instrument touches
the second moment of the transition cost. A carbon price (a Weitzman price instrument) moves the
mean; a contract (a quantity-certainty instrument) removes the variance; and because the irreversible
position is priced off variance, the contract dominates for premium reduction. We contribute the
irreversibility channel to the prices-vs-quantities tradition.

7.2 Irreversible investment under uncertainty (Dixit–Pindyck; Pindyck; Fuss)
The real-options literature (Pindyck 1991; Dixit and Pindyck 1994) established that irreversibility plus
uncertainty creates a value of waiting that raises the investment threshold above the Marshallian
trigger. Fuss et al. (2008) applied this to energy investment under carbon-price uncertainty, finding
that policy uncertainty delays low-carbon investment. Our paper both uses and qualifies this tradition.
We use its machinery — the American-option formulation and the Longstaff–Schwartz solution
(Longstaff and Schwartz 2001) — but we show (§4.6) that the sign of the timing response to variance is
model-specific and, in the steel calibration, runs opposite to the naïve reading: because conversion is
triggered on low-price realizations, variance advances rather than delays the exercise date. We
therefore relocate the robust claim from the exercise threshold to the risk premium, where the sign is
unconditional. This is a methodological contribution to how real-options results should be stated when
the timing comparative static is ambiguous: price the risk, do not time the option.

7.3 The mean-preserving spread (Rothschild–Stiglitz)
The link from a contract's variance removal to the premium runs through the Rothschild–Stiglitz
(1970) mean-preserving-spread order: reducing σ_B is a mean-preserving contraction of the transition
cost, which lowers the value of a convex option and — under the forced-exercise boundary that flips the
sign — lowers the liability premium. We use the MPS order as the exact justification for why the
premium responds monotonically to variance while the mean instrument does not.

7.4 Carbon-transition risk pricing (Bolton–Kacperczyk)
Bolton and Kacperczyk (2023) established empirically that a carbon premium is priced in global equity
returns — firms with higher emissions earn higher returns, compensating for transition risk. Their
result is a cross-sectional level: emissions predict returns. Our contribution is orthogonal and
complementary: we do not re-estimate the premium's level but decompose it into the four drivers
whose variance generates it, and we prove (Proposition 1) that the decomposition is invariant to the
market price of risk that Bolton–Kacperczyk estimate. Where they identify that transition risk is priced,
we identify what it is priced on and which instrument retires each component. The two results
compose: their λ is our premium's scale; our shares are its composition.

7.5 What the paper corrects
The paper's own earlier framing — and the intuitive reading of the transition-finance literature — held
that the policy goal is to advance the decarbonization decision and that contracts do so by closing a
timing wedge. The numerical model refutes the timing form of that claim (§4.6, §6). We correct it: the
defensible and validated statement is that contracts lower the cost of capital on the irreversible
investment, which makes the rebuild financeable at a lower hurdle. This is a subtler claim than
"contracts move the date," but it is the one the model supports, it matches the observed pricing of
transition risk, and it survives the full budget-tightness robustness sweep. Stating it correctly is part of
the contribution.
